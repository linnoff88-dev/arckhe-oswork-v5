Track 01 · Lesson 1

Complete request,
no loose question

At the end of this lesson, you can turn a loose question into a complete request — with material, output format, and definition of done — and get back something you use without rewriting.

There’s a preferred time for rework: the end of the afternoon, when you already spent the good part of your day and you get back a text that needs to be redone entirely. Almost always, the cause is what you left out when you asked — and what you left out you only discover after reading the answer. This lesson reverses that order.

↓ scroll to study

01 The AI starts every conversation without knowing anything about your work

When you open a new conversation, on the other side there’s nobody who knows your school, your team, or last week’s agreement. There’s a system that reads what you wrote in that box and nothing else. Anything you don’t tell it, it fills in using the average of what it has already seen in millions of texts — and the average is always generic.

This set of information you provide along with the request has a name: it’s the context. It’s not decoration for the request. It’s the raw material for the work.

A teacher asks “make a summary of yesterday’s class” and receives a text about the topic in general, usable for any class in the country. They didn’t paste their own lesson plan, they didn’t say the class is seventh grade, and they didn’t warn that the summary is for those who were absent. None of that was in the box — so none of it showed up in the answer.

02 Material, input, and definition of done — what the complete request needs to include

When someone leaves a car at a shop, they don’t just say “take a look.” The work order records what the owner heard, what they want resolved, and when they need the car back. That’s what lets you work without interrupting the owner every time a question comes up. A work request for an AI carries the same three pieces of information.

The input is what she needs to read: the original text, the numbers, the example of how you want it. The output is the form of the response: how many lines, what tone, and who will read it. The definition of done is what must necessarily appear for you to accept the result.

A manager needed to turn a 30-page safety standard into the schedule for the team’s mandatory training. Instead of asking “build a schedule for this,” she pasted in the standard, said “a table with four columns: topic, time needed, audience, and month,” and ended with “it’s only done if it covers the five topics required by the standard.”

Before

“Build a training schedule based on this standard.”

After

Standard pasted · four-column table: topic, time needed, audience, and month · it’s only done if it covers the five required topics.

Balance: from four rewrites for an adjustment — about twenty minutes back at each round.

03 You decide what a good result looks like

Of the three parts, the one almost everyone forgets is the third. We remember input and format because they’re visible. The definition of done stays in our head — and what stays only in our head doesn’t make it to the other side.

The definition of done is a sentence that lets you answer yes or no without debate. “It’s good” isn’t a definition, it’s a wish. “It needs to cite the three decisions” is a definition: you include three, or you don’t.

A history teacher who corrected eighty student submissions per grading period wrote his definition once: each submission needs to point to a specific correct thing from the student’s text and one single thing to improve, in up to four lines, with no generic praise. With the definition up front, you can check the response in seconds.

Test yourself

You asked for “support material about the new rule for the team” and got a correct, well-written text — that nobody used. What was missing from the request?

04 Before you send it to be executed, ask for what’s missing

There’s a 30-second step that avoids most of the rework: before you send someone to do it, ask what still needs to be done to do it well. Write your request and close with one line — “before executing, tell me what’s missing for you to deliver this the right way.”

The answer is usually a short and uncomfortable list: the audience is missing, the deadline is missing, the original text is missing. Exactly what you would have found after reading a bad answer—only now you discover it before, while the topic is still fresh in your mind.

A manager used this line before asking for the notice about the new expense reimbursement rule. The answer asked which month the rule applied from, and whether it covered people who had already requested reimbursements in that month. Both pieces of information were indeed missing—and they would have generated an incorrect notice sent to the entire team.

AI doesn’t guess what you didn’t write.

Track 01 · Lesson 2

The checking
ruler

At the end of this lesson, you can check an AI response in less than two minutes, using a three-item checking ruler that you write yourself before you ask.

The wrong answer arrives with exactly the same tone as the right answer: clearly written, organized, no hesitation. Without a short annotated list before you ask, you check by the feeling that it sounds well written. And that is the only sign the tool always gets right. The cost shows up when the wrong number is already out in the delivered material.

↓ scroll to study

01 The confident tone of the reply says nothing about whether it is correct

A text AI does not keep a file of truths to look up. It builds the next sentence by choosing the most likely word after the previous one. When the material exists, the likely word is usually the correct one. When the material is missing, the likely word keeps showing up — only now it fills the gap with something plausible.

That is why the made-up information never comes with a warning. It arrives in the same firm sentence, with the same punctuation and the same calm as the rest. You will not notice it by reading: both versions look like they are correct.

A geography teacher asked for a support paragraph about the watershed of a Brazilian river. It came back as a clean text, with the length in kilometers and the year of a historic flood. The length was correct. The year of the flood was off by two — and nothing in the text separated one from the other.

Test yourself

Two responses about the same material arrived clearly written and disagree on a value. What signal clears up the doubt?

02 Write the three items before you send the request

Checking later, on the fly, almost always turns into a lazy reread. The way out is to note beforehand what you will hold them to. That is the checking ruler: three lines, written while the topic is still fresh.

One, the fact you already know by heart — the bait. If it comes out wrong, the rest does not deserve to be read. Two, the number or name that must match the source, checked one by one. Three, what cannot appear: no statement that is not in the delivered material.

A manager would set up the team’s vacation board. Before requesting it, she wrote down three lines. The balance of days for herself, which she knows by heart. The colleagues’ departure dates, to match the spreadsheet. And the prohibition on citing a rule outside the pasted document.

Before

Read the entire answer twice, looking for something odd, and send it anyway when nothing catches your eye.

After

Three lines written before the request: the bait you already know, the numbers that need to match, and what can’t show up.

Balance: about eight minutes of unsafe rereading for a two-minute check, with a clear stopping point.

03 You switch tools; the checking ruler stays the same

There are several AI conversation tools, and a new one appears every few months. They change the screen, the name, and the price. What doesn’t change is the way all of them build the sentence — and, with it, the chance of a plausible fact slipping in without warning.

That’s good news for you: the checking ruler you wrote doesn’t age with the tool. The same three-line list works in today’s tool and in the one your school will adopt next year. You learn to check once, not once per tool.

A literature teacher changed tools because the old one became slow. In the first week, the new one delivered a reading list with a title that that author never wrote. He caught the error in item two of the same checking ruler: the titles had to match the library’s catalog.

04 Checking is the task; deciding about someone stays with you

The checking ruler handles the checkable part: number, name, date, citation. There’s another part that no list covers, because it’s not about accuracy. It’s about consequences in a person’s life.

A text can have all the correct data and still propose something you wouldn’t agree to. Ask for the write-up, accept the draft, check the items — and keep to yourself the final sentence that affects someone. This part isn’t slow due to carelessness: it’s slow because it’s yours.

A manager used the tool to organize records of absences for an entire semester. The numbers were right and checked. The paragraph that suggested dismissing one of the people she deleted on the first reading. That conclusion doesn’t come from a ready-made text.

Confidence in tone isn’t proof of anything.

Find the made-up fact in two texts that look the same

Ready when you compare your answer to the answer key, item by item. About 12 min.

This case is fictional and stays here on the page: nothing is sent, nothing is saved, and nobody sees what you wrote. If your answer doesn’t match the answer key, read its comment and go back to the material — getting it wrong here is the goal of the exercise.

Sílvia coordinates the support services of a school and received a cleaning proposal. She pasted the proposal into two AI tools and asked for the same thing: a four-line summary to take to management. Both texts came back well written. One of them includes a condition that does not exist in the proposal.

PROPOSAL (the source material)
Aurora Cleaning · 3 visits per week · R$ 4,200 per month
12-month contract · annual adjustment by the price index

TEXT A
Aurora Cleaning offers three weekly visits for R$ 4,200
per month, in a 12-month contract with annual adjustment. It also includes
two extra visits per quarter, at no additional cost.

TEXT B
Proposal from Aurora Cleaning: three visits per week,
R$ 4,200 per month, 12-month contract, and annual adjustment
by the price index. No other conditions are described.
Annotated answer key

Which text fails: the Text A. The two extra visits per quarter are not in the proposal. Notice that it’s the best-written text of the two — the extra sentence is exactly what makes it look more complete at first glance.

Which item catches: the third — what cannot appear. The numbers in Text A match the proposal in every point, so items one and two pass. Only the ban on quoting what was not delivered knocks the sentence down.

What Sílvia does: she uses Text B, or deletes the extra sentence from A. And she doesn’t ask the tool to confirm whether the condition exists: it confirms it in the proposal, which is the source.

Track 01 · Lesson 3

A folder
for context

At the end of this lesson you have, on your computer, a folder per piece of work, with the material that the AI needs to read — and that you know how to say what never belongs in there.

Today the material on a subject of yours is scattered. One part is in your email, another on your desktop, another inside an old attachment. That’s why you restate everything again in each new conversation. This isn’t your lack of memory — it’s the lack of a single place where the whole subject lives.

↓ scroll to study

01 A subject, a folder — and no “folder by file type”

I call it piece of work any subject that shows up again in your week and has a life of its own. Each piece of work deserves its own folder, on your computer, with the material for that subject gathered together.

The rule is simple: the folder is organized by the subject, not by file type. You don’t want one spreadsheet folder and another for texts. You want one folder where, when you open it, the whole piece of work is there — the text, the number, and the agreed plan.

A manager opened a folder called “junior analyst hiring”. Inside it were the job posting description, the three interview questions, and the approved salary band table. Before that, each of those three materials was in a different place, and she restated the job at every new conversation with the AI.

02 Inside the folder goes what you would have had to explain out loud

You will see a common folder, just like any other on your computer, with three or four files inside. Nothing here is different from what you use every day — only the rule for who gets to enter changes. The rule is this: what you would have had to explain out loud if a new colleague took over the piece of work tomorrow.

In practice, this usually gives you four things. The source material: the text, the table, the notes. An example of the result you consider good. The rules of the place where that will be used. And the decision already made that nobody should reopen.

A math teacher set up the second-semester recovery folder. She included the list of covered topics, an old test he corrected, and the coordination’s guidance on the minimum grade. With those three, each new exercise request comes out at the right level without him rewriting the agreed plan.

Common mistake

Put everything in and wait for the AI to choose. The folder turns into a storage bin: twenty versions of the same document, the abandoned draft, last year’s material. It will read everything with the same weight and be responsible for the wrong version. Keep the version that matters today and send the old ones to a folder called “antigo” inside the same tray.

03 Personal data and a password never go in—no exceptions, even if you’re in a rush

Anything that’s in the folder can end up being read by a system from outside your home. That’s why there’s a short list of what never goes in: another person’s ID document, home address, health data, and account number. And any password or access code, including yours.

When the material only serves with names, replace each name with a nickname before you save: Student A, Team Member B. You keep the meaning of the case and remove anything that identifies someone. It takes a minute, and there’s no going back once the data leaves.

A manager was going to save the team performance evaluation spreadsheet to ask for help writing the comments. The spreadsheet had full name, document number, and salary. She saved a second copy with Team Member A, B, and C, without the document and salary columns. Only that copy went into the folder.

04 Ten minutes per month keeps the folder standing

Any living folder quickly fills with clutter. Not because you’re disorganized, but because the work keeps moving: yesterday’s version becomes today’s wrong one. Without a short, marked cleanup, in two months the folder goes back to being the storage bin it came to fix.

Mark ten minutes at the end of the month and go through the same sequence every time. The goal isn’t to make it look nice. It’s to make sure that when you open the folder, everything inside is still true.

A biology teacher does this on the last Friday of the month, in the science fair folder. In ten minutes, he removed two expired regulations and renamed the schedule for the new date. The draft invitation that had already been sent went down into the “antigo” folder. The folder ended up with five files, all valid.

The sequence below is the same every time, and no step requires a hard decision. You just look, decide if it’s true today, and move whatever isn’t.

The ten-minute cleanup

  1. Open the folder and look at one file at a time. Without opening everything: the name and date already tell you almost everything.
  2. Ask about each one: is this still true today? If it isn’t, it can’t stay in the first level.
  3. Send anything that has expired to the “antigo” folder. Don’t delete—just move it out of the way.
  4. Rename what’s left with a confusing name. Use subject and month: “September schedule,” not “final v3 new”.
  5. Check the list of what never goes in. If any personal data slipped into the folder during the month, take it out now.

Practice now 0/4 done

Build the folder for one of your work fronts now

Ready when you open a single folder and can see, without looking around elsewhere, the three materials that explain the front. About 12 min.

Nothing here gets deleted. Moving a file does not destroy anything: it leaves one place and appears in another. Your computer itself undoes the last move if you change your mind. If something seems wrong, stop and undo—none of the steps in this practice affects anything outside your computer.

You just gathered an entire work front in one place. Inside it is the material the AI needs to read, and none of the things that should never leave your hands.

Track 01 · Lesson 4

The orientation sheet
from project

At the end of this lesson you have an orientation sheet written for one of your work fronts, so you do not need to repeat its rules with every request.

You repeat the same three or four requirements in every new conversation: keep the formal tone, do not make up numbers, sign as coordination. In calm days you remember them all. On a busy day you forget one, and it is exactly on that day that the text comes out wrong and goes out. The folder from the previous lesson stores the material, but it does not store any of those rules.

↓ scroll to study

01 Your rules fit on a single sheet, written once

Think about who covers your vacations. You don’t call every day: you leave a sheet with your house combinations, and the person looks it up on their own. A orientation sheet is that sheet, for the work ahead.

She keeps it inside the folder you set up in the lesson before, along with the materials. Whenever you start a new conversation about that work front, you attach the sheet before asking for anything. Your rules arrive complete, and you don’t have to type any of them again.

Before you see the sheet from the inside, in the practice of this lesson, a translation. What you’ll see is a plain text, in Portuguese, written in your own way — no special screen and no code. In technology teams, this same sheet is often called an AGENTS.md. The name is his; the content is yours, and you only change the sentences.

A purchasing manager repeated two requirements in every conversation. Supplier treated by company name, and a deadline always with a date on the calendar. She wrote both lines once and stopped typing them.

Common mistake

Fill the materials sheet instead of rules. Many people paste the entire contract inside it, the month spreadsheet, the meeting transcript. The sheet gets bulky, and the rule that mattered gets lost in the middle.

The division is simple: materials go loose in the folder, the rule goes in the sheet. If the sentence changes every week, it’s materials. If it applies to everything that comes out of there, it’s a rule.

02 Four questions, and the sheet is written

A good sheet isn’t a long sheet. Four questions cover almost everything you repeat today by memory, and each answer fits in two lines.

Who reads what comes out of here sets the tone and vocabulary. How I write keeps your mark: sentence length, greeting, signature. What can never appear is the hard limit: a person’s name, a number you didn’t verify, a contract value. How I know it’s ready is the criterion you already use since the very first lesson.

Answer each one with something you can verify. “Be clear” doesn’t work, because nobody can say whether it was followed. “Sentences of up to twenty words” does work: you can count.

A science teacher wrote the reminder sheet that goes in families’ agenda. Who reads it: guardians, many with little time. Never can appear: student name and individual grade. Ready when: it fits in one paragraph and ends with the date.

03 There’s a rule of yours that always applies, and a rule that’s only for that work front

After the second sheet is written, you notice you copied the same lines in both. Those are your general rules: they apply to everything you produce, in any work front. They deserve their own sheet, saved one level above the folders.

Project rules stay in the sheet of each folder, and only apply there. When the two overlap, the project one wins — it’s the one closest to the work. Writing that once avoids the discussion for every request.

A manager generally has the rule of never sending a number without the source next to it. In the board’s monthly report, there is one more rule, only for that: always compare with the same month from the previous year, never with the last month.

Separating the two layers

  1. Read each line from your orientation sheets out loud. One at a time, no rush.
  2. Ask: does this apply to every front of work I run? Respond yes or no, with no in-between.
  3. Yes becomes the general rule. Cut out the line and move it to the general sheet, outside the folders.
  4. It doesn’t stay where it is. That’s a rule for that front of work and it doesn’t interfere with the others.
  5. When in doubt, treat it as a project rule. A rule that’s too narrow costs little; a rule that’s too broad cuts across work that has nothing to do with it.

04 The sheet gets old — mark the date and revise

The sheet describes a job that changes. The team reorganizes, the class changes, the board asks for a different format. The sheet stays the same, followed to the letter, pushing you into a way of working that is already over.

The fix costs little: write the revision date on the first line and come back to it when the work changes shape. Read line by line and ask if it’s still true. A rule that no longer holds goes away; it isn’t marked as old—it’s removed for real.

A teacher put together the recovery study-lesson roadmap in March, with the morning class. In August, they took over the night class, with students who work. The line “propose reading for the next day” turned into an impossible demand, and they only realized it after three strange roadmaps.

Test yourself

You wrote the front-desk service sheet, and three months later, the answers start coming out with a treatment the team had abandoned. What’s the most likely cause?

Track 01 · Lesson 5

The template
that you reuse

At the end of this lesson, you have a request template saved for the task that repeats most in your month, ready to use by changing only what changes.

Every week you rewrite almost the same request from scratch. Since you write from memory, the result changes depending on the day: on a calm Tuesday you get something good; on a rushed Friday you get only half. The task is the same; what varies is how fast you are when you ask. You fix that once, and it stays fixed.

↓ scroll to study

01 What you redo every month is already asking for a template

There’s a task in your month that always comes back the same in form and different in content. The numbers change, the people change — but the type of work is the same. That’s the natural candidate to become a template.

A template is the complete request you learned in Lesson 1, only written all at once and saved. You don’t redo it: you open it, change what changes, and send it. The decision of how to ask was made on a day when you were calm, and it keeps applying on the days when you’re not.

Every Friday, a manager creates the department’s metrics report for the board. The four indicators, the audience, and the size are always the same. Only the week’s numbers change. She rewrites this request forty times per year.

Common mistake

Keep the response that came out good, instead of keeping the request that produced it. It’s the natural reflection: the nice text is what’s on the screen, so it’s what goes into the folder. But that text works for that situation, and for no other.

What you reuse isn’t the result. It’s the recipe that created it.

02 Separate what never changes from what changes with each use

Making the template is making a separation, and that’s all. Take the last real request for this task and put a line in each row: does this apply every time, or only this time?

What applies to every time is written out in full and is never typed again. It’s your role, the audience, the tone, the size, and the rules you always repeat. What applies only to this time becomes a blank: a marked space, with a short example of what goes there. A blank without an example is too open, and the answer comes out different with each use.

A teacher prepares the recovery practice questions list every two months. Fixed: twelve questions, from the simplest to the hardest, with an answer key explained in two lines. Blank: the bimestre’s content and the two errors that showed up the most on the test. He wrote this once.

Test yourself

You built a template and used it twice. The two answers came out with very different sizes and tones. What’s the most likely cause?

03 The definition of done lives inside the template

A template that only has material and format saves typing, but it doesn’t save checking. The part that gets most tiring with repetition is deciding, every time, whether the result works.

Write that decision once, inside the template, in two lines: what the answer absolutely needs to include, and what it can never do. With both in place, checking turns into counting instead of opinion.

A service manager used to answer each complaint by hand as it came in through the site’s form. In her template, the required line says: you need to repeat the problem using the customer’s words, give a deadline with a date, and sign off through the department. The forbidden line says: never promise a refund of value. Checking dropped to twenty seconds per response.

Building your template

  1. Open the last real request you wrote for this task — the real one, with its mistakes, not a cleaned-up memory version.
  2. Mark each line as fixed or variable, asking whether that applies every time or only to this one.
  3. Transform each variable line into a blank and write a short example of what goes there beside it.
  4. Add the two criteria lines: what the answer must have and what it can never do.
  5. Use two times in a row with different content, and correct what was ambiguous in the first reading.

04 Save where your hand will find it on Friday

A template you can’t find in ten seconds is a template you don’t use. It lives in the front-work folder of Lesson 3, next to the orientation sheet. Not left on the desktop, and not lost in an old conversation.

Give it a name that describes the task, not the content: “template — weekly report for management” is right; “good text version 3” is not. Many chat apps also save reusable text in a menu called library, saved, or favorites. If yours has it, use both places: the folder is your copy, the app is the shortcut.

A teacher writes recommendation letters for former students who request them throughout the whole year. She saved the template in the school folder named “template — recommendation letter”. When a request comes in November, she opens it, changes the student name and the period, and delivers it the same day.

Track 01 · Lesson 6

Don’t lose
what already worked

At the end of this lesson, you can change a work material while knowing you can go back to the exact previous version, without relying on remembering what changed.

You tweak a rule in your orientation sheet to improve a small detail, and the result of the following week comes out worse than it was. When you try to undo it, you realize you saved over the good version. This wasn’t carelessness: you were missing a return point, and no one tells you it’s necessary until the day it becomes necessary.

↓ scroll to study

01 Every improvement in a material that already works is a bet

When you change a rule in a material that was already producing results, you’re betting that the new one will be better. Sometimes it is. Other times, the change fixes the detail that was bothering you and breaks something that had been working quietly without you noticing. You only find out on the next use, days later.

The problem isn’t betting — betting is how the material improves. The problem is betting without a return point. Without it, a bad bet costs the whole reconstruction of what you already had.

A manager adjusted the time-off rule in the shift schedule spreadsheet to cover a holiday. The holiday schedule came out right. Two weeks later, the same adjusted rule left two Saturdays in a row with nobody — and the old version of the spreadsheet had already been saved over.

Before

Open the material, change the rule, save. The version that worked stops existing at the exact same moment.

After

Duplicate the material with today’s date in the name, change the rule in the original file, save. Both versions exist.

Balance: fifteen seconds more per change, instead of rewriting from scratch in half an afternoon when the change goes wrong.

02 The copy with the date in the name, made before you touched anything

The point of no return fits in one action: before you change any material that already works, duplicate the file and put today’s date at the beginning of the copy’s name. That’s it. You end up with two files in the same folder: the one you’ll change and the one that stays frozen in the good state.

Always use the same way of writing the date, from largest to smallest: year, month, and day. Written this way, the computer sorts the copies for you, from the oldest to the most recent. You can see the timeline just by looking at the folder.

A math teacher keeps the orientation sheet for recovery exam questions. Before loosening the minimum grade requirement, they duplicated the file and named the copy with that day’s date. The new criterion turned out to be too lenient. They opened the copy, read the old rule, and put back the exact sentence. It took less than a minute, without trying to remember anything.

03 Write down the reason, because what changed in the material already shows it

Saving the copy solves half. The other half is knowing why you changed it. In three months, with the two files open side by side, you can see on your own which sentence came in and which one went out. What neither of them keeps is the reason. And it’s the reason that stops you from undoing a well-made decision months later.

So the record is short, and it has only one thing: the reason. One line per change, with the date, in the same place every time. A notebook works; an notes file works. The criterion is to be able to write it in fifteen seconds—otherwise you drop it in the second week.

A manager changed the minutes template for the purchasing committee. She recorded: “I removed the estimated value field because two suppliers received the minutes by mistake.” Four months later, someone suggested putting the field back. The line answered in two seconds what no one’s memory could.

The material shows what changed. Only you keep the why.

04 One line per mistake, written on the day it happens

The same notebook gets a second type of line: the mistakes. Every time a result comes out wrong and you fix it, write the date, what came out wrong, and the smallest fix that solved it. One line, no story. Write it on the day, not at the end of the month, because at the end of the month you’ve already forgotten the fix.

Isolated, these lines seem useless. In ten or twelve lines, they stop feeling that way. What shows up is repetition: the same kind of error coming back through different paths. Then you stop fixing the result of that day and correct the rule that produces the error. It’s usually a sentence missing from your sheet.

A science teacher documented for a month the flaws in the lab worksheet generated with AI. Three lines said the same thing: the safety warning for heated material was missing. They stopped adding the warning by hand every week and wrote the requirement in the orientation sheet, once.

You changed a rule in your orientation sheet two weeks ago, and the result got worse. You have the dated copy saved, but you didn’t note anything. What did you lose?

Track 01 · Lesson 7

What you delegate
and what you keep doing

At the end of this lesson, you have your full AI environment set up and a written list of what you delegate, what you check, and what you don’t hand off to anyone.

The pieces are already there, each in its right place. What’s missing is the decision about where the delegation ends. Without that written line beforehand, it gets drawn right in the middle of a busy day — and then the choice almost always ends up on the wrong side. Deciding calmly today takes fifteen minutes and pays off for months.

↓ scroll to study

01 You delegate the execution, never the responsibility

A task has two parts that need to be separated here: the work of producing what gets made, and the fact that someone is accountable for the outcome. The AI can take the first part. The second one is still yours, completely, even when you didn’t write a single word of what was sent.

That’s why this course calls this supervised delegation. The name is literal: you hand over the doing and you keep hold of the answering. If you don’t separate the two, you only find out on the worst possible day.

A manager at a call center asked for the monthly indicator report for the board. The text came well assembled, and she sent it without reading the last table, which switched reopened tickets for resolved ones. In the meeting, the one who explained the mistake was her — and she was right that it would be.

02 Every task fits one of three levels

There is no delegating or not delegating. There are three positions, and most tasks change places over time. Do it yourself is what you accept without looking, because an error there costs almost nothing. Do it and show it is what comes out ready and goes through your eyes before moving on. Only suggests is what you keep doing with your own hands, using the response as just a first draft or as a list of ideas.

The position doesn’t depend on the AI being good at that. It depends on who signs and what happens if it goes wrong.

A biology teacher put the review exercise list in do it and show it: he reads the ten questions before printing. The welcome text from the room’s bulletin board ended up in do it yourself. The bimonthly assessment ended up in only suggests, because the student’s grade depends on it.

How to classify a task with three questions

  1. Who signs. If the result comes out under your name or goes outside the team, it doesn’t go into do it yourself.
  2. How much the error costs. If wrong information there creates rework for other people, it moves up to do it and show it at least.
  3. You can check it quickly. If you can’t verify the result in a few minutes, the task stays in only suggests until you have a checking ruler that fits that time.

03 The time to review counts toward your earnings

The account most people make is too simple: the task took forty minutes, the answer came back in one, so the earnings were thirty-nine. You still miss a share. Reviewing costs time, and that time comes out of the same day.

An honest account has three parts: write the complete request, review the answer, and adjust what came back crooked. When the sum of the three gets close to the time it takes to do it by hand, the delegation gives back nothing. It only shifts the effort to a different place, with one extra risk.

An operations manager moved the vacation schedule for the team to AI. Putting it together took twenty minutes; reviewing holidays, shift coverage, and the day limit took eighteen. She brought the schedule back to her own hands and delegated the email that goes with it, which takes fifteen minutes to write and two minutes to review.

04 The list stays true only if it has a date

Your list grows old fast, and for a good reason: you get better at asking. A task that looked simple in January can be ready to do by May. The opposite is also true, and that’s the case that hurts more.

That’s why the list needs a fixed time in your calendar, once a week, fifteen minutes. In that meeting, you upload what tested well, drop what failed twice, and note the why in one line. Without a marked date, nobody comes back: the list becomes a nice paper, written once and never read again.

Rosana, a manager of an appointment-setting service, put everything together in one month. She created the front-desk folder, wrote the orientation sheet with the department’s rules, and saved the template for the rescheduling notice. In the fourth week, the notice went out with a deadline that was already past, and the dated copy sent back the good version in two minutes. The line she wrote afterward — the deadline changed, the sheet didn’t follow — became the first item on the next review checklist.

A geography teacher scheduled his review for Friday at 4:00 PM, right after the last lesson. In fifteen minutes, he revisits the list, reviews two materials from the week, and adjusts one rule on the sheet. In four weeks, two tasks moved up a level and one moved down.

05 What you have now, together, is an environment

It’s worth naming the parts. You write a complete request, with material, format, and definition of done. You have a three-item checking ruler, written before you ask. You have a folder for each work front. You have an orientation sheet with your rules. You have a template for the task that repeats the most. And you have the dated copy, which guarantees the way back.

Separated, they’re six loose habits. Together, with the delegation list on top, they’re an environment. Each request starts in a folder, follows an orientation sheet, comes out of a template, goes through a checking ruler, and has a return point. You can explain that to someone else in five minutes — and that’s the measure that it’s set up.

A project office manager used exactly this to welcome a new person to the team. She handed over the folder, the orientation sheet, and the template on day one, and instead of two weeks of scattered questions, the onboarding took three days. What she passed on wasn’t a tool: it was the written agreement.

  • Teacher: start with what turns into a grade. Exercise bank in does and shows, support material in does alone, exam correction in only suggests.
